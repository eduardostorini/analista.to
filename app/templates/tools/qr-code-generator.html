What is the QR Code Generator?

A QR code (Quick Response code) is a two-dimensional barcode that packs text — a website URL, a phone number, a Wi-Fi password, plain text, almost anything — into a pattern of black and white squares that any modern smartphone camera can scan and decode instantly. This tool generates a QR code entirely on our server from whatever text or URL you enter, at a pixel size you choose from a short list of common presets, and gives you a PNG file you can download and use immediately — on a printed flyer, a business card, a product label, a presentation slide, or anywhere else a quick scan is more convenient than typing. Nothing about what you encode is stored publicly or shown to other visitors: this is the one tool on the site that intentionally does not keep or publish a result page, precisely because what people put into a QR code is often personal or private.

How to choose a size

Size should match how the code will be viewed and how far away the scanner will be. A smaller size like 150 or 200 pixels works well embedded in a digital document or a web page meant to be scanned from a phone held close by. Mid-range sizes around 300 to 500 pixels are a safe default for most printed material, like flyers or product packaging, scanned from a comfortable arm's length. Larger sizes — 800 or 1000 pixels — are meant for large-format printing, posters, or storefront signage that needs to be scannable from several meters away, since a QR code's readable distance scales roughly with its printed size. Whatever size you pick, the underlying pattern is generated at full sharpness and scaled without blurring, so the code stays crisp and scannable rather than becoming a soft, unreadable smear the way a simple photo resize often does.

How to interpret the result

The image you get back is a complete, standalone QR code — there is nothing further to configure or verify beyond checking that your phone's camera can read it back correctly, which is worth doing before printing something at scale. Longer text needs more modules (the small black-and-white squares that make up the pattern) to encode, so very long input produces a visually denser code that can be harder to scan reliably at small sizes; if you need a QR code for a long piece of text, prefer a larger preset size, or consider shortening the content — a short URL, for instance, encodes far more reliably than a long one with many tracking parameters attached.

Common use cases

QR codes bridge printed and physical materials with digital destinations: a restaurant menu linking to an online ordering page, a business card linking to a contact file or LinkedIn profile, event signage linking to registration, a product label linking to a manual or warranty page, or a shared Wi-Fi network's credentials made scannable instead of typed character by character. They are also commonly used for quick internal tooling — encoding a server hostname, a short internal note, or a one-time link — where speed and simplicity matter more than a polished design.

Limitations

Text over roughly 1,500 characters is rejected outright, since QR codes become impractically dense and unreliable to scan well before reaching their theoretical maximum capacity — if you need to encode a large amount of text, host it at a URL and encode the link instead. This tool does not support custom colors, embedded logos, or error-correction level selection; it favors a plain, maximally scannable black-on-white code over visual customization. Finally, remember that a QR code is just a container — it does not make the destination it points to any safer or more trustworthy, so treat scanning an unfamiliar QR code with the same caution you would an unfamiliar link.
