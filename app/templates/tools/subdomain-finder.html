What is Subdomain Finder?

Most organizations run far more subdomains than the handful listed on their homepage — staging environments, internal dashboards, regional sites, API endpoints, and old marketing microsites frequently live on subdomains that are never linked from anywhere public. Subdomain Finder discovers these by searching Certificate Transparency logs, a public, append-only record that every trusted certificate authority is required to publish to whenever it issues a TLS certificate. Since virtually every subdomain running HTTPS today has a certificate — and certificates commonly cover multiple subdomains at once through Subject Alternative Names — these logs end up being an unusually complete public map of an organization's HTTPS-enabled infrastructure, discoverable without ever touching the target's own servers.

How to interpret the result

Each entry in the list is a hostname that appeared on at least one publicly logged certificate for the domain you searched, at some point in time. This does not mean every listed subdomain is currently live — a certificate can still be logged even after the server behind it has been decommissioned, moved, or replaced, since Certificate Transparency logs are permanent historical records, not a live inventory. A large number of subdomains, especially ones with generic names like dev, staging, test, or old, often signals forgotten infrastructure that may no longer be maintained or monitored as closely as production systems — exactly the kind of asset that security reviews look for first. To check whether a specific subdomain is actually active, follow up with the DNS Lookup or Ping tool.

Common use cases

Security teams and penetration testers use subdomain enumeration as one of the very first steps of reconnaissance, since mapping the full attack surface of an organization — not just its main website — is essential before any deeper testing begins. IT and security staff run it against their own domains periodically to catch forgotten or unauthorized subdomains before an attacker does, a practice often called "shadow IT" discovery. Competitive researchers and marketers use it to understand how a competitor structures regional sites, product lines, or internal tools. It is also a useful sanity check after a company reorganizes its infrastructure or migrates providers, confirming that old subdomains were properly retired rather than left dangling and pointing at infrastructure nobody controls anymore — a common source of subdomain takeover vulnerabilities.

Limitations

This tool is entirely passive: it never scans, brute-forces, or connects to the target domain's own infrastructure, which means it can only find subdomains that have had a publicly logged TLS certificate at some point. Internal-only subdomains that never obtained a public certificate, or organizations that rely exclusively on broad wildcard certificates without individual entries, will not be fully represented. Conversely, since Certificate Transparency logs never expire entries, the list can include subdomains that were decommissioned years ago — treat every result as a lead worth verifying, not a guarantee that the subdomain is currently live, resolvable, or still controlled by the same organization. For a complete assessment, combine this list with active checks like DNS Lookup, Ping, or the Website Hosting Checker.
