What is DMARC?

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting & Conformance) is an email authentication standard published as a TXT record at _dmarc.<domain> that ties together the results of SPF and DKIM and tells receiving mail servers what to do when a message fails both — and, critically, whether the domain shown in the message's visible "From" header actually matches the domain that was authenticated. Without DMARC, SPF and DKIM only prove that a specific sending server or signing key was authorized; they say nothing about whether the address a human recipient actually sees was the one that passed. DMARC closes that gap by requiring "alignment" between the authenticated domain and the visible one, and by giving domain owners a policy (p=) to instruct receivers on how strictly to enforce it, plus a reporting address (rua=) to receive daily summaries of every message claiming to be from the domain — authenticated or not. The {{ site_name }} DMARC Checker fetches this record, parses every tag, and flags configuration choices that leave a domain exposed to spoofing or that were never tightened beyond the initial "monitoring only" phase.

How to interpret the result

The p= tag is the core of the policy: none means the domain is only being monitored — spoofed mail is still delivered normally to inboxes, with reports sent back to the owner for visibility; quarantine asks receivers to treat failing mail as suspicious, typically routing it to spam; reject asks receivers to refuse failing mail outright at the SMTP level. The optional sp= tag overrides this policy specifically for subdomains, useful when a domain sends mail from many subdomains with different trust levels. pct= (default 100) applies the policy to only that percentage of failing messages, letting teams ramp up enforcement gradually instead of switching straight from monitoring to full rejection. adkim= and aspf= control alignment strictness: "s" (strict) requires an exact domain match, while "r" (relaxed, the default) accepts a match on the organizational domain, allowing subdomains to align with the parent.

Common use cases

This tool is used to confirm a domain is actually protected against direct-domain spoofing before relying on that assumption — a surprising number of domains, including well-known ones, publish DMARC only at p=none indefinitely, which provides reporting but zero enforcement. It is also used mid-rollout, when a team is deliberately raising pct= in stages from a small percentage toward 100 while watching aggregate reports for false positives, and as a pre-migration check before switching email providers, since a new provider's messages need to pass SPF or DKIM in alignment with the visible From domain or they will be caught by the very policy the domain owner published.

Limitations

This tool validates the syntax and structure of the DMARC record itself; it cannot tell you whether a specific message you received actually passed DMARC — for that, inspect the Authentication-Results header of the message with the Email Header Analyzer. It also does not evaluate the aggregate (rua) or forensic (ruf) reports a domain receives, which is where the real evidence of spoofing attempts and legitimate-but-misaligned senders (like a forgotten marketing platform) actually shows up over time — those reports need to be collected and reviewed separately, often through a dedicated DMARC monitoring service.

Rolling out DMARC safely

Moving straight to p=reject without first monitoring aggregate reports is a common and costly mistake — it is easy to forget a legitimate sending source (an old CRM, a support ticketing tool, a payroll provider) that has been sending mail on the domain's behalf without proper SPF/DKIM alignment for years. The recommended path is to publish p=none first with a working rua= address, review the reports for a few weeks to identify every legitimate sender, fix alignment for each one, then move to p=quarantine at a low pct=, and only reach p=reject at pct=100 once reports show no more legitimate failures. This tool is meant to be used repeatedly through that process, not just once.
