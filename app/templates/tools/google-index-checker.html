What is the Google Index Checker?

Search engines maintain a massive catalog of publicly reachable web pages called the index. When Google discovers a page through links, a sitemap, or a manual submission, it may add it to that catalog so it can appear in search results. The Google Index Checker estimates how many pages from a given domain are currently in Google's index by querying the `site:` operator and reading the result count Google displays on the search results page. This is useful for SEO audits, content migrations, and troubleshooting situations where you expect a site to be well represented in search but suspect pages are missing, excluded by robots.txt, or blocked by accidental noindex tags. Because Google does not expose an exact count through a public API, this tool provides the closest practical approximation available without signing up for Google Search Console access.

How to interpret the result

The number shown is the approximate count returned by Google for the query `site:yourdomain.com`. A larger number generally means Google has found and stored more pages from that domain, which is a positive signal for visibility. A smaller number than expected can indicate crawl budget issues, thin content, hosting or DNS problems, manual penalties, or recent migrations where old URLs were dropped before new ones were discovered. If you see zero results, the domain may be entirely new, completely blocked by a robots.txt rule, deindexed, or simply unreachable by Googlebot at the time of the check. The result is also influenced by regional and personalization factors, so rerunning the check from a different location or after a few hours may yield a slightly different figure. Always treat this number as a directional signal rather than an exact inventory, and confirm suspicious changes with Google Search Console, which provides authoritative coverage, crawl errors, and indexed-page history over time.

Common use cases

SEO professionals use this tool during site migrations to confirm that the new domain is being discovered and indexed at a healthy rate after launching new content or changing CMS platforms. Agency teams use it in client reports to show whether SEO efforts are expanding or shrinking a site's presence in Google's catalog. Support teams use it to diagnose sudden drops in organic traffic by checking whether the index count collapsed at the same time as rankings fell. Developers use it after deploying a new robots.txt or noindex configuration to verify that the change had the intended effect without waiting for manual checks in Search Console. Marketers use it to benchmark competitors by comparing indexed page counts across similar domains, which can reveal whether a rival is publishing at a much higher velocity or maintaining a much larger content footprint. It is also useful before buying or selling a website, because an unexpectedly low index count can be an early warning of hidden penalties, orphaned URLs, or poor site architecture that would require expensive cleanup after acquisition.

Limitations

Google does not provide an exact, real-time index count through its public interface, so the number shown is an approximation based on the search results page. The figure can change from one query to the next because of regional data centers, A/B testing, logged-in personalization, and temporary crawl throttling. If a domain uses a CDN, redirects, or canonical tags, the count may reflect only the canonical version Google has chosen, not every URL that exists on the origin server. Very large domains may show a capped or rounded number instead of a precise total. Sites with aggressive anti-bot measures may return incomplete results or trigger temporary challenges. Finally, this tool only checks Google; it does not report indexation on Bing, Yandex, or other search engines, which may have different coverage, crawl schedules, and policies. For a complete picture, combine this tool with sitemap validation, robots.txt inspection, and Google Search Console coverage reports.
