What is DMARC?

DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance) is the standard that ties SPF and DKIM into a single coherent policy, published as a TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.com. It solves a problem that SPF and DKIM alone do not fully address: what to do when an email fails authentication, and who should be notified when that happens. Without DMARC, each email provider decides on its own how to handle messages that fail SPF/DKIM; with DMARC, the domain owner explicitly defines the policy (ignore, quarantine/spam, or reject) and starts receiving regular reports about who is sending email on behalf of your domain — including spoofing attempts.

How to interpret the result

The policy (p=) is the most important field: none only monitors, blocking nothing — this is the recommended starting point before advancing to stricter policies; quarantine directs failing messages to the spam folder; and reject instructs receiving servers to completely reject unauthenticated messages — the strongest protection, but it should only be activated after confirming, via aggregate reports, that all legitimate senders are properly authenticated. The pct field allows applying the policy to only a percentage of messages, useful for a gradual and safe transition to reject. The absence of an address in rua= means the domain is not receiving any aggregate reports — in practice, giving up the main visibility source that DMARC provides.

Common use cases

This tool is used to confirm that a domain is protected against spoofing before a simulated phishing campaign or a security audit, to track the progress of a domain gradually migrating from p=none to p=reject, and to diagnose why legitimate emails from a partner domain are being rejected by third parties (frequently because SPF/DKIM are not aligned with the configured DMARC policy). Email marketing providers and security teams also review DMARC regularly as part of brand protection against email spoofing.

Limitations

This tool checks only the syntax and presence of the DMARC record — it does not evaluate whether the domain's SPF and DKIM are actually aligned with the policy (which can only be confirmed by analyzing headers of real delivered emails, or the aggregate reports received over time). A well-configured DMARC depends on SPF and DKIM being correctly implemented — review both configurations together for effective protection.

Deployment best practices

Safe DMARC adoption typically follows a gradual path: first publish a record with p=none and a valid rua= address, letting the domain collect aggregate reports for at least two to four weeks. Analyze those reports (manually or with a specialized tool) to identify all legitimate sending sources, including third-party services like marketing platforms, CRMs, and support systems, which are frequently forgotten at this stage. Only after confirming that SPF and DKIM are aligned for all those sources does it make sense to advance to p=quarantine and then to p=reject, ideally using the pct field for a gradual transition instead of a sudden change that could block legitimate emails not yet identified.
